Alt-Az Tracking with an EKF, Compared with PHD2, MountWizzard4 and TPoint

PHD2, MountWizzard4, TPoint and HYBRID address different parts of the tracking and pointing problem. This article compares their approaches — feedback guiding, mount model building, pointing analysis and an Extended Kalman filter — with a focus on alt-az mounts.

Alt-azimuth mounts are harder to use for long-exposure astrophotography than equatorial mounts. An equatorial mount aligns one axis with Earth's rotation, which reduces tracking to a single constant speed. On an alt-az mount, both axes move at variable speed, field rotation turns the image across the sensor, and atmospheric refraction bends starlight by an amount that changes with altitude. The common solution is to use an equatorial mount, or an equatorial wedge. HYBRID instead models these nonlinear effects and handles them with an Extended Kalman filter.

The Alt-Az Challenge

Field rotation is the most visible problem. As an alt-az mount tracks a star across the sky, the field of view rotates around the optical axis. Stars do not trace straight lines across the sensor — they trace arcs. After just a few minutes of uncorrected tracking, stars become elongated streaks at the edges of the frame even while remaining sharp at the center. This effect is absent on equatorial mounts where the RA axis is aligned with the celestial pole.

Both axes must move simultaneously at variable speeds. On an equatorial mount, the RA axis spins at the sidereal rate — a constant 15.041 arcseconds per second — and the Dec axis sits still. On an alt-az mount, both altitude and azimuth change continuously, and the rate of change depends on where in the sky you are pointing. Near the zenith, azimuth speed approaches infinity. Near the horizon, atmospheric refraction becomes significant and nonlinear.

Atmospheric refraction compounds the problem. Stars near the horizon appear higher than they actually are, and the correction follows a secant-of-zenith-angle law — not a simple linear offset. The refraction coefficient changes with temperature, pressure, humidity, and wavelength. A static correction table gets you within a few arcseconds, but it cannot track the real-time drift that occurs as conditions shift through an imaging session.

Meridian crossing introduces a change of behavior. At the meridian the altitude axis reaches its maximum and reverses direction, while the azimuth rate peaks — very sharply for targets that pass close to the zenith. For targets crossing the northern meridian, azimuth values also wrap through 0°/360°. Controllers that do not account for these effects can produce tracking spikes or lose the guide star.

A common solution is to use an equatorial mount and avoid these effects. HYBRID's approach is to model the nonlinearities explicitly and let the Extended Kalman filter handle them.

The 4 Approaches

PHD2: Feedback Guiding

PHD2 is an autoguiding application. Each guide frame, it measures the offset between the guide star and its reference position and sends a correction through one of several selectable guide algorithms — such as hysteresis, resist-switch, low-pass and Predictive PEC. These act much like the proportional, integral and filtering terms of a classic feedback controller, so this article groups them under the PID label. The approach is simple, well understood and widely used.

For equatorial mounts, this works very well. The system is approximately linear: the mount moves at a constant speed on the RA axis, and corrections are small perturbations around that rate. In this regime PHD2 routinely achieves sub-arcsecond guiding.

On alt-az mounts, a guider covers only part of the problem. PHD2 keeps a guide star at its reference position; it does not model field rotation, which on alt-az systems is usually handled by a field derotator or by keeping exposures short. Refraction and geometry effects reach it as part of the measured error rather than as separately modeled terms. Most of its algorithm parameters stay fixed during a session, although the Predictive PEC algorithm learns the periodic component of the error as it runs.

PHD2 is designed for guiding, not for generating the tracking rates themselves. It corrects small errors around the mount's own tracking. On alt-az mounts, those base tracking rates come from the mount controller or another program.

MountWizzard4: Mount Model Building

MountWizzard4 is an open-source companion application for 10Micron mounts. It automates building the mount's model: it slews to a series of points across the sky, plate-solves each one, and transfers the results to the mount. The model is then applied by the mount's firmware during pointing and tracking.

For mounts it supports, this is an effective way to reduce systematic errors. A model built from many plate-solved points captures alignment, flexure and other repeatable error terms, and well-modeled mounts can track unguided for long exposures.

The model describes repeatable errors. It is calibrated in a dedicated run and does not re-estimate itself from measurements during the imaging session. MountWizzard4 is also tied to 10Micron hardware, and refraction correction is applied by the mount firmware from temperature and pressure data rather than estimated from the tracking measurements. It is a modeling tool rather than a closed-loop tracking controller.

TPoint: Polynomial Fitting

TPoint fits a polynomial surface to pointing measurements. By collecting dozens or hundreds of calibration points across the sky, TPoint builds a model of the mount's systematic pointing errors, including flexure, gear errors, and alignment offsets.

For pointing accuracy, TPoint is a long-established reference. After a thorough calibration run it can achieve arcsecond-class pointing across the sky, and its models capture spatial patterns that simpler models miss. It is used on amateur and professional telescopes, including alt-az instruments.

A TPoint model is fitted from a calibration run and then applied as a fixed correction; it does not re-estimate itself from measurements during the session. Building it requires many calibration points — typically 30 to 100 — collected over one or more hours. TPoint itself is a pointing-analysis tool; when its model is used by telescope control software, for example by ProTrack in TheSkyX, it also improves tracking, because the tracking rates are derived from the corrected pointing.

HYBRID: Extended Kalman Filter

HYBRID uses an Extended Kalman filter with a 6-element state vector: RA position, RA velocity, Dec position, Dec velocity, atmospheric refraction coefficient, and field rotation rate. This state captures the essential dynamics of alt-az tracking in a compact, computationally efficient form.

The EKF's Jacobian linearizes the nonlinear mount geometry at each time step. This handles the altitude-dependent azimuth speed, the secant-of-zenith-angle refraction law, and the field rotation geometry without requiring a globally linear model. The linearization is recomputed at every cycle, so it adapts automatically as the target moves across the sky.

EKF State Vector: x = [ RA_pos, RA_vel, Dec_pos, Dec_vel, Refraction_coeff, Field_rotation_rate ] Prediction Step: x_predicted = f(x_previous, dt) // Nonlinear motion model P_predicted = F * P * F' + Q // Jacobian linearization Update Step: K = P_predicted * H' * (H * P_predicted * H' + R)^(-1) // Kalman gain x_updated = x_predicted + K * (z - h(x_predicted)) // Measurement update P_updated = (I - K * H) * P_predicted

The refraction coefficient in the state vector means the filter learns atmospheric conditions from the data itself. No external weather model is needed — the Kalman gain adjusts the refraction estimate based on how well the prediction matches the measurements. The field rotation rate is estimated directly from plate-solve solutions, so no external field rotation model is required.

A machine learning layer tunes the noise covariance matrices (Q and R) based on 24 features including seeing conditions, mount temperature, sky position, and session history. This layer engages when model confidence exceeds an R² threshold of 0.5, allowing the filter to optimize its own noise parameters without manual tuning.

A feedback guider corrects the error it measures in the current frame. The EKF also predicts the error for the next cycle from its motion model, and estimates refraction and field rotation as part of its state. Design notes

Why HYBRID Uses an EKF for Alt-Az

Several properties of the Extended Kalman filter suit alt-az tracking. The Kalman gain balances prediction against measurement: when the model is reliable, the filter weights its prediction more; when measurements are noisy, it relies more on the model. This balance is optimal in the minimum-variance sense when the model and noise assumptions hold. In practice the noise parameters still have to be chosen, which is the role of the ML layer.

The Jacobian linearization handles nonlinear terms at each step without requiring a globally linear model. The secant-of-zenith-angle refraction law, the altitude-dependent azimuth speed, and the field rotation geometry are all nonlinear functions that the Jacobian linearizes locally. This differs from treating the whole system as linear and relying on the errors staying small. The linearization is itself an approximation and becomes less accurate where the geometry changes fastest, such as near the zenith.

The state includes the refraction coefficient directly, so the filter estimates atmospheric refraction from the tracking data. It can therefore run without a weather station, although weather data can still be used by the ML layer. As temperature and pressure change through the night, the refraction estimate follows. The field rotation rate is estimated from plate-solve solutions, so no separate field rotation model is required.

Manual tuning is reduced. The Kalman gain adjusts the filter's behavior to the observed noise, and the ML layer further adjusts the noise covariance matrices based on patterns learned in the current session. The filter starts producing estimates from the first plate solve and refines them as measurements accumulate; it needs several cycles to converge, but no separate calibration session is required.

Where Other Tools Fit Better

Each of the other tools is the better choice for some tasks. For equatorial guiding, PHD2 is simpler, well established, and has a large user community and extensive documentation. If you use an equatorial mount and do not need alt-az tracking, PHD2 is the practical choice.

For 10Micron mounts, MountWizzard4 is the specialized tool. It builds and manages the mount's model, which the firmware then applies during pointing and tracking, and it can make unguided imaging practical on those mounts.

For pointing models, TPoint is a long-established reference on amateur and professional telescopes. With dozens of calibration points it reaches a pointing accuracy that a tracking filter is not designed to provide. If your main need is accurate pointing across the sky, TPoint is the right tool.

HYBRID is aimed at alt-az tracking with plate-solve feedback and ML noise tuning. It models refraction and field rotation explicitly and adapts during the session. It is a newer project with a much smaller user base than the other tools, and its published results come from the project's own testing.

Real-World Results

The figures below compare reported tracking performance. They describe different quantities and setups, so they are indicative and should not be read as a controlled side-by-side test. On equatorial mounts, HYBRID's Kalman tracking measured 0.5 to 1 arcsecond RMS, compared with typical PHD2 guiding results of 0.3 to 0.8 arcsecond RMS. PHD2 reaches the lower error in this case; the EKF adjusts its weighting automatically, while PHD2's algorithm parameters are set by the user or with its Guiding Assistant.

On alt-az mounts, HYBRID's EKF tracking measured 1 to 2 arcseconds RMS with field rotation correction active. The other tools do not report a directly comparable figure, because they do not target alt-az field rotation in this way and their published numbers measure other things. The ±2 arcseconds shown for MountWizzard4 covers only the modeled error component, and the ±30 arcseconds shown for TPoint is pointing accuracy across the sky, not tracking error.
